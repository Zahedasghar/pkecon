The Central Puzzle


In 1978, China was poorer than most sub-Saharan African countries

  • Poorer than Chad, Bangladesh, Malawi
  • Recovering from 30 years of Maoist upheaval
  • No private property rights
  • No rule of law
  • No democratic institutions

The Puzzle
Conventional development theory says you need good institutions first — democracy, rule of law, property rights — before markets can grow.

China had none of these and yet grew for 40+ consecutive years to become the world’s second-largest economy.

The Parable of the Pauper and Two Gurus

🧙 First Guru — “Growth First”
“Earn your first paycheck. Once you earn money, your circumstances will improve and you will escape poverty.”

Problem: How does a poor person get the first job without education, address, or connections?

🧙 Second Guru — “Institutions First”
“Do what my rich clients do: attend college, move to a safe neighbourhood, buy health insurance.”

Problem: All these prerequisites require wealth you don’t yet have.


Both advice falls short. Attaining the preconditions of wealth also depends on the level of economic wealth. So how do poor, weak societies escape poverty traps?

Three Competing Theories of Development


Theory 1
Growth → Institutions

Modernisation theory: As countries grow rich, a middle class demands accountability and rights.

Harrod-Domar: growth from capital investment; Sachs: aid from rich nations

Theory 2
Institutions → Growth

World Bank / IMF consensus: First build a checklist of “best practices” — rule of law, property rights, democracy. Then markets will follow.

Why Nations Fail (Acemoglu & Robinson) — inclusive institutions

Theory 3
History → Institutions → Growth

Path dependence: Favourable historical legacies created strong institutions that enabled prosperity.

“Once society gets organised a certain way, this tends to persist”


Ang’s critique: All three theories treat development as a linear, one-directional process. None can explain China — or most of the developing world.

Why Standard Explanations Fail for China


Conventional Explanation Why It Falls Short
“Inclusive, democratic institutions” China has authoritarian, one-party rule — yet sustained 40 years of growth
“Growth will eventually slow” True for everyone — not an explanation
“Plenty of catch-up room” Then why haven’t other poor countries caught up?
“Cheap labour + foreign capital” Many countries offer this — they didn’t grow like China
“Bureaucratic incentives for growth” China is huge — incentives alone can’t explain it
“Confucian culture of hard work” Culture is roughly constant — can’t explain 1978 as the turning point
“Sheer luck” Forty years of sustained growth by luck?


Ang’s argument: These explanations each capture a piece of reality but no single one — or combination — explains the process by which China moved from poverty to prosperity.

Ang’s Core Argument

Development is not a linear process.
It is a coevolutionary one.

Markets and institutions do not have a fixed causal sequence. They mutually constitute each other — changing and adapting in response to each other over time, in a process that is neither linear nor predetermined.

“The economy and the bureaucracy interact and change together… The government can’t reform overnight.”

The Chicken-and-Egg Problem — Dissolved


The conventional framing creates a false dilemma:

Good Governance /
Strong Institutions
→ ?
← ?
Economic Growth /
Functioning Markets


Ang’s answer: Both arrows operate simultaneously — through a coevolutionary process

The key insight: You do not need strong institutions to begin building markets. You need institutions that are good enough — and adapted to the specific level of development — to serve as a foundation. Markets then generate resources that allow institutions to strengthen, which in turn enables more complex markets.


“Spark change in poor societies by leveraging what they already have — not with what we wish they could have.”

The Three-Step Coevolutionary Sequence


1

Harness existing weak institutions to build markets
Poor countries already have some institutions, even if weak, informal, or corrupt. The task is not to wait for perfect institutions but to leverage what exists — redirecting bureaucratic incentives, local authority, and informal norms toward facilitating exchange and investment.

2

Emerging markets stimulate stronger institutions
As markets grow and generate wealth, new demands arise for more reliable property rights, contract enforcement, and regulatory capacity. Economic actors invest in improving institutions because they now have resources and incentives to do so.

3

Stronger institutions preserve and deepen markets
Once markets reach sufficient complexity, stronger formal institutions become essential. By this stage, the economy has generated the surplus to finance them and the political will to demand them.

The Blind Men and the Elephant


Ang uses the parable of blind men describing an elephant to explain why all existing models of China are partially correct but collectively incomplete.


  • The “democracy causes growth” scholar sees one part
  • The “institutions-first” economist sees another
  • The cultural-Confucian theorist sees yet another


Each description is accurate from its vantage point. None captures the whole animal.

Complexity ≠ Complicated
China is not simply a very complicated system that requires very complicated models. It is a complex adaptive system — emergent, self-organising, path-dependent, but not random.

Ang’s method: Instead of connecting snapshots (cross-sectional data), trace the process through which China’s economy and institutions changed each other over time — using both historical depth and provincial-level variation within China.

What Made 1978 the Turning Point?


Not: A sudden adoption of Western institutions Not: Discovery of a new natural resource Not: Foreign aid or geopolitical windfall


The Household Responsibility System (HRS)
Deng Xiaoping allowed villages to keep surpluses above state quotas. This used the existing collective institution — production teams — to create private incentives. A weak institution was redirected, not replaced.

Township and Village Enterprises (TVEs)
Hybrid organisations — collectively owned, market-oriented — that operated outside state planning. Not private firms, not state firms: a transitional form suited to China’s weak property rights environment.


The lesson: Neither reform was textbook “best practice.” Both leveraged existing conditions rather than imposing transplanted institutions.

Directed Improvisation: China’s Bureaucratic Model


Two components working together:

D
Directed
Central government sets broad performance targets — especially GDP growth — and holds local officials accountable via the cadre evaluation system.
I
Improvisation
Local officials have enormous discretion to achieve targets using whatever means suit local conditions. This produced enormous variation in strategy across provinces.

Why this matters
This is not “corruption” or “chaos” — it is a deliberate administrative design that allows a country as vast and heterogeneous as China to adapt policies to local realities rather than imposing uniform rules from Beijing.


The trade-off
Directed improvisation also generates corruption, inequality across regions, and policy inconsistency. Ang explicitly acknowledges these costs — they are not bugs, but features of the transitional model.

Three Stages — What “Good Enough” Institutions Look Like


Stage Economy Institutions Needed China’s Equivalent
Early Subsistence / barter Basic order, property norms, local exchange Village collectives, party cadres
Middle Emerging markets Enforceable contracts, credit, infrastructure TVEs, special economic zones, informal courts
Advanced Complex global markets Formal rule of law, IP protection, independent judiciary Still evolving — ongoing challenge


Ang’s key insight: The institutions appropriate for one stage are not the same as those needed at the next stage. Transplanting advanced-stage institutions into early-stage economies does not accelerate development — it disrupts the coevolutionary process.

The Implication for Development Policy


The standard prescription (World Bank, IMF, “good governance” agenda):

Adopt a comprehensive package of formal institutions — property rights, independent judiciary, anti-corruption agencies, democratic elections — as a prerequisite for growth.


“The imposition of good governance standards has been a root cause of the deep problems encountered by developing countries.” — Pritchett & Woolcock

Ang’s alternative prescription:

Identify the institutions that are good enough for the current stage of development. Design interventions that redirect existing institutional capacity toward market-building, rather than waiting for ideal institutions to materialise.


Focus on the sequence and conditions for adaptation — not on replicating any single country’s institutional model.

Why China’s Model Cannot Be Simply Replicated


What appears to be “the China model”:

  • Strong central party control
  • Government-directed investment
  • SOE dominance in strategic sectors
  • Suppressed political opposition


But none of these is the actual mechanism. China is a large, evolving elephant — it changes colour and size constantly.

What IS replicable — in principle

The logic of coevolution:
Start with what you have → create conditions for markets to emerge → let market-generated resources strengthen institutions → repeat at the next level of complexity.

What varies by country
The specific starting institutions, the path of adaptation, the pace of change, and the political constraints — all differ. There is no universal blueprint.

Connecting to Pakistan: What Does This Framework Reveal?


Pakistan’s conventional reform narrative:

“We need good governance / rule of law / anti-corruption reforms first — then investment will come, growth will follow.”

Result: 25 IMF programs, each restoring macrostability without structural transformation. The chicken-and-egg trap persists.

The coevolutionary question instead:

  • What institutions does Pakistan already have that can be redirected toward market-building?
  • Which local-level incentives can be restructured without waiting for national reform?
  • Where has improvisation worked in Pakistan — SEZs, IT sector, remittance corridors — and why?


The lesson from Ang is not “copy China.” It is: find your own coevolutionary path — building on what exists rather than transplanting what does not.

Key Takeaways


1
Development is coevolutionary, not linear. Markets and institutions build each other simultaneously — there is no fixed causal sequence.
2
Weak institutions are not an excuse for inaction. They are the starting point. The task is to redirect them, not wait for ideal ones to appear from outside.
3
Stage-appropriate institutions matter. Institutions suited to one level of development are not the same as those needed at the next. Transplanting advanced-stage rules into early-stage economies disrupts rather than accelerates development.
4
Local variation and directed improvisation are features, not bugs. Uniform top-down prescriptions cannot accommodate the diversity of conditions across regions and stages.
5
The question is not “which model to copy.” It is “what conditions enable a society to continuously adapt its institutions as its markets grow in complexity.”

Discussion Questions


1. Ang argues that China used weak institutions to build markets first. Can you identify any sector in Pakistan where this logic has operated — even partially?

2. The “good governance first” prescription has been applied to Pakistan through 25 IMF programs. Using Ang’s framework, what is the fundamental problem with this approach?

3. Ang distinguishes between “complicated” (requiring complex models) and “complex” (adaptive, emergent). Why does this distinction matter for how economists study development?

4. China’s coevolutionary path involved significant corruption, regional inequality, and political repression. Is this a necessary cost of the model, or a feature that can be avoided?
