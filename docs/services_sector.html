Understanding the Services Sector

Definition, classification, and why it matters for Pakistan

Services create value without producing a physical good — and they drive Pakistan’s modern economy

  • Definition: Services are intangible economic activities — banking, retail, transport, education, health, ICT — where output is consumed as it is produced

  • Also called the Tertiary Sector; it follows from agriculture (primary) and manufacturing (secondary) in the structural transformation ladder

  • PBS classifies services into: Wholesale & Retail Trade · Transport & Storage · ICT · Finance & Insurance · Real Estate · Public Administration · Education · Health · Other Private Services

  • Services differ from goods in four ways — Intangibility (cannot be touched), Inseparability (produced and consumed simultaneously), Variability (quality varies), and Perishability (cannot be stored)

Source: Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, National Accounts Methodology; Ministry of Finance

Four Sub-Sectors Shaping Pakistan’s Future

IT & Telecom · Banking & Finance · Formal vs Informal · Education & Health

Pakistan’s IT and telecom sectors are growing rapidly — but remain far below their potential

  • Telecom: ~200 million subscriptions by March 2025; sector revenues Rs 803 billion (Jul–Mar FY25); mobile data usage jumped 68.9% YoY to 4.3 GB per subscriber

  • IT Exports: Reached ~$3.2 billion annually; Pakistan ranks among the world’s top-5 freelancing nations by earnings

  • Startups: Over 1,900 startups incubated under National Incubation Centres (NICs); 185,000+ jobs created; Rs 30.8 billion in investments; 12,000 women entrepreneurs empowered

  • Government Push: e-Rozgaar targeting 50 active centres by FY2025; China–Pakistan JWG for ICT, cybersecurity, and 5G cooperation

  • Constraint: High duties on mobile devices, rupee depreciation (−17% in 2024) inflating spectrum fees, and limited 5G rollout throttle competitiveness

Source: Pakistan Economic Survey 2024–25; SBP; Mordor Intelligence Telecom Pakistan Report 2026

Banking is modernising fast — but financial exclusion leaves most Pakistanis outside the formal economy

  • Finance & Insurance contribute ~4% of GDP but function as the nerve centre of investment, credit, and economic confidence

  • SBP cut policy rates by 450 basis points to 17.5% through FY2025 — easing credit and spurring private investment growth of 9.9%

  • Mobile banking surge: JazzCash alone has 48 million registered wallets; mobile banking transactions hit 1.69 billion in FY2025 — financial inclusion from the bottom up

  • Capital markets: PSX market capitalisation surged 50% to Rs 10.2 trillion; KSE-100 gained 78,000 points; corporate bond issuance up 38% to Rs 480 billion

  • Key gap: Only ~35% of adults hold bank accounts; 72% of non-agricultural workers are in the informal economy without access to credit, insurance, or pensions

Source: Pakistan Economic Survey 2024–25; SBP Annual Report; Express Tribune June 2025

Pakistan’s services sector is split: a growing formal economy alongside a vast informal one

Formal Services (Registered & Taxed)
  • Banks, telecom firms, listed companies
  • Pays taxes, counted fully in GDP
  • Regulated by SBP, SECP, PTA
  • Workers have contracts, benefits, pensions
  • Accessible to foreign investment & credit
Informal Services (Unregistered & Untaxed)
  • Street vendors, informal retail, rickshaws
  • Largely invisible to GDP measurement
  • No regulatory oversight or consumer protection
  • Workers lack social safety nets
  • 72.1% of non-agricultural employment — the majority
Source: Labour Force Survey 2024–25; Wikipedia Economy of Pakistan 2025; Bank of Scotland Trade 2024

Challenges Holding the Services Sector Back

Six structural barriers limiting growth, jobs, and tax revenue

Six structural barriers prevent Pakistan’s services sector from reaching its full potential

1
Low Tax Base
Services = 58% of GDP but contribute a disproportionately small share of taxes — most providers operate outside the formal tax net
2
Skills Gap
Education spending is only 2.1% of GDP (UNESCO benchmark: 4–5%); workforce skills do not match what services employers need
3
Digital Divide
Internet growth is uneven — rural areas and women remain largely excluded from digital services, e-commerce, and online work
4
Energy Crisis
Utility tariffs rose 29% in FY2025; erratic power supply inflates operational costs for service firms and reduces competitiveness
5
Regulatory Burden
Complex registration, high telecom taxes, and bureaucratic red tape deter formalisation and discourage foreign direct investment
6
Informality
72.1% of non-agricultural workers are informal — shrinking the tax base and leaving most service workers without social protection
Source: Pakistan Economic Survey 2024–25; World Bank June 2025; Mordor Intelligence 2026; PBS Labour Force Survey

Opportunities for Growth

Digital economy · Youth dividend · Remittances · CPEC · Tourism

Demographics, geography, and digital momentum create a genuine window for services-led growth

✦
Youth Dividend
53.8% of Pakistan is working-age (15–59); with the right skills this demographic bulge can power IT, fintech, education, and health services
✦
IT & Freelancing
$3.2 billion in IT exports; English-speaking, young tech talent offers a competitive cost advantage in the $7 trillion global services market
✦
Remittances $32bn
Remittances grew 11% to $32 billion in FY25 — the largest forex source — sustaining household consumption and retail services demand
✦
CPEC Connectivity
CPEC enhances logistics, transport corridors, and cross-border trade; CPEC-II adds digital and green corridor components
✦
Financial Inclusion
Mobile wallets (JazzCash: 48mn users) and SBP's digital push can bring millions of unbanked Pakistanis into formal financial services
✦
Tourism Potential
Pakistan's scenic North, cultural heritage sites, and hospitality sector offer significant growth if security and infrastructure improve
Source: Pakistan Economic Survey 2024–25; SBP; Mordor Intelligence; World Bank 2025

Five reforms would unlock the services sector’s true potential and make it an engine of inclusive growth

  1. Formalise the Informal: Simplify business registration, offer tax amnesties, and use digital payment data to bring service providers into the tax net — widening the base without raising rates

  2. Invest in Human Capital: Raise education spending toward 4–5% of GDP; expand vocational and digital skills training aligned with what services employers actually need

  3. Deepen Financial Inclusion: Use SBP’s digital infrastructure and mobile money platforms to extend credit, insurance, and savings products to the unbanked majority

  4. Reform Telecom Taxation: Reduce duties on mobile devices and dollar-linked spectrum fees to accelerate 5G rollout, smartphone adoption, and the digital economy

  5. Promote Services Exports: Strengthen PSEB, invest in IT parks, offer R&D incentives, and negotiate bilateral services trade agreements to capture global market share

Source: IMF Article IV 2024; World Bank Pakistan Development Update 2025; Pakistan Economic Survey 2024–25

Key Takeaways

1
Services account for 58.4% of GDP and employ 38% of Pakistan's workforce — making it the single most important sector in the economy today
2
Wholesale & retail trade (30.8%), transport (18.2%), and real estate (10.1%) are the three largest sub-sectors; IT/telecom and finance are the fastest-growing
3
A massive informal economy — 72% of non-agricultural employment — is Pakistan's biggest structural challenge: it shrinks the tax base and denies workers social protection
4
Pakistan's IT sector ($3.2bn exports, 200mn telecom subscribers, 1,900+ incubated startups) is a genuine growth engine constrained by high taxes, skills gaps, and energy costs
5
The way forward requires five parallel reforms: formalisation, human capital, financial inclusion, telecom tax reduction, and a deliberate services export strategy

Pakistan’s services sector is its largest —
but not yet its most productive.
That gap is the reform opportunity.
